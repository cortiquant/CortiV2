CortiQuant 
Mind Stress Index (MSI) — v3 
Instrument Design, Scoring Logic, and Scientific Credibility Report 
This document details the final 16-item Mind Stress Index (MSI v3), the reasoning behind its structure, 
the scoring formula and matrix used to derive a composite stress score, and the scientific validation 
design intended to establish its credibility as a self-report stress-screening instrument. 
  

=== PAGE 2 ===
1. Overview of the MSI v3 
The Mind Stress Index (MSI) is a 16-item self-report instrument developed by CortiQuant to screen for 
workplace and academic stress. It is organised into four subscales, each capturing a distinct dimension 
of the stress experience: 
• Mood — recent emotional state (past week) 
• Stress Load — perceived pressure, control, and overload (past month) 
• Recovery & Support — ability to bounce back and access to social support (past month) 
• Physical — bodily/somatic symptoms of stress (past week) 
Each subscale contains 4 items, chosen as the minimum item count that permits a statistically stable 
estimate of internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha) while keeping respondent burden low. All items 
are written in plain, single-clause language (under 10 words each) and use a uniform 5-point frequency 
response format, with the exception of one emoji-based mood item retained for engagement and 
ease of response. 
2. Final Item List 
Mood (reference period: past week) 
• M1 — "How's your mood been?" (emoji scale: 뇤뉤뉥눷 뇤눨눩눪눫눬 덞덛덟덜덝 뇤뇪뇫뇥뇦 뽹뽸뽺뽻뽼) 
• M2 — "I've felt low or down." 
• M3 — "I've felt worried or on edge." 
• M4 — "I've struggled to feel calm or relaxed." 
Stress Load (reference period: past month) 
• S1 — "I've felt nervous or stressed." 
• S2 — "I've felt unable to control important things in my life." 
• S3 — "I've felt like I had too much to handle." 
• S4 — "Small problems have felt bigger than they are." 
Recovery & Support (reference period: past month) 
• R1 — "It's taken me a while to feel okay after something stressful." 
• R2 — "I've struggled to relax even when I had time to." 
• R3 — "I haven't had anyone to talk to when things got hard." 
• R4 — "I've felt overwhelmed by everyday tasks." 
Physical (reference period: past week) 
• PH1 — "I've felt tense or on edge in my body." 
• PH2 — "I've had headaches or tight shoulders/neck." 
• PH3 — "I've felt tired even after resting." 
• PH4 — "My stomach or sleep has been off." 

=== PAGE 3 ===
Response options (M2–M4, S1–S4, R1–R4, PH1–PH4): Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Often / Always. All 
items are worded uni-directionally — higher frequency always indicates greater distress — eliminating 
the need for reverse-scoring on 15 of the 16 items. 
3. Design Logic 
3.1 Why four subscales 
Stress is not a single homogenous experience; established models of stress (e.g., transactional stress 
theory, Lazarus & Folkman, 1984) distinguish between the emotional, cognitive-appraisal, 
physiological, and coping/resource dimensions of the stress response. The four MSI subscales map 
onto this structure: Mood (emotional), Stress Load (cognitive appraisal), Physical (physiological), and 
Recovery & Support (coping/resource buffering). 
3.2 Why four items per subscale 
Cronbach's alpha, the standard measure of internal consistency, becomes statistically unstable below 
3 items and cannot be meaningfully interpreted with only 1–2 items. Four items per subscale was 
selected as a safety margin above this minimum, balancing measurement reliability against 
respondent burden — the full 16-item MSI is designed to be completed in under 3 minutes. 
3.3 Why items echo gold-standard phrasing 
Several items (e.g., S1, S2, S3, R1, PH1, M4) were deliberately worded to closely parallel language used 
in established, validated instruments (PSS-10, DASS-21, WHO-5, BRS — see Section 5). This is a 
recognised technique in scale development to strengthen convergent validity, since closely related 
wording increases the likelihood that both instruments are capturing the same underlying construct. 
Items not mirrored on a gold standard (e.g., S4, R2, R4) were retained to preserve MSI's distinct 
measurement contribution beyond simple replication of existing tools.

4. Scoring Logic and Formula 
4.1 Item coding 
All items except M1 (Never=1, Rarely=2, Sometimes=3, Often=4, Always=5): 
M1 is reverse-coded, since it is the only positively-framed item: 뇤뉤뉥눷=5, 뇤눨눩눪눫눬=4, 덞덛덟덜덝=3, 뇤뇪뇫뇥뇦=2, 뽹뽸뽺뽻뽼=1. 
This ensures all 16 items point in the same direction (higher = more distress) before scoring. 
4.2 Subscale raw scores 
Subscale Formula Raw Range 
Mood M1(recoded) + M2 + M3 + M4 4–20 
Stress Load S1 + S2 + S3 + S4 4–20 
Recovery & Support R1 + R2 + R3 + R4 4–20 
Physical PH1 + PH2 + PH3 + PH4 4–20 
  
4.3 MSI Total raw score 
MSI Total (raw) = Mood + Stress Load + Recovery & Support + Physical    
[Range: 16–80] 
4.4 Composite score (0–100 scale) 
The raw total is converted to a 0–100 composite score using a linear transformation: 
MSI Composite = ((Total Raw − 16) / 64) × 100 
The same linear transformation is applied at the subscale level: 
Subscale Composite = ((Subscale Raw − 4) / 16) × 100 
4.5 Why a linear formula was chosen over a compressed/curved transformation 
An earlier design consideration explored a logistic (sigmoid) transformation, which would 
mathematically guarantee the composite score never reaches exactly 0 or 100 — modelling the 
biological reality that physiological stress arousal is never truly absent nor infinite. While this is a 
defensible product-communication idea, it was deliberately excluded from MSI's validation scoring for 
one critical reason: all four gold-standard comparator instruments used in MSI's validation (PSS-10, 
DASS-21, WHO-5, BRS — see Section 5) use simple linear summation with no curve or compression. 
Correlating a curved MSI score against linearly-scored comparators would distort the resulting 
correlation coefficients, particularly at the score extremes, and would introduce a methodological 
inconsistency that undermines — rather than strengthens — the credibility of the validation. The 
linear formula is therefore used for all scientific validation and correlation analysis. A compressed 
display transformation (e.g., capping the range shown to end-users) may be considered separately as 
a product/UX decision once validation is complete, but this is explicitly a presentation-layer choice, 
not a scoring or scientific one, and should not be described as part of MSI's validated scoring 
methodology.

5. Scientific Credibility — Validation Design 
MSI's scientific credibility rests on a concurrent validity design: each subscale is validated against an 
established, published instrument measuring a closely related construct, administered to the same 
respondents in the same sitting. 
MSI Subscale Gold Standard Expected Direction Expected r 
Mood WHO-5 Well-Being Index Negative −0.35 to −0.55 
Stress Load PSS-10 (Perceived Stress 
Scale) 
Positive 0.45 to 0.65 
Recovery & Support Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) Negative −0.30 to −0.50 
Physical DASS-21 Stress Subscale Positive 0.35 to 0.55 
MSI Total PSS-10 (headline figure) Positive 0.40 to 0.55 
  
Interpretive benchmarks (Cohen, 1988): r = 0.10–0.29 weak, 0.30–0.49 moderate, 0.50–0.69 strong, 
0.70+ very strong. A target range of r = 0.40–0.65 is considered ideal for convergent validity between 
related-but-distinct instruments — high enough to indicate genuine construct overlap, but not so high 
as to suggest MSI is merely redundant with existing tools. 
5.1 Internal consistency targets 
Cronbach's alpha will be calculated for MSI Total and each of the four subscales. The conventional 
acceptability threshold is α ≥ 0.70; α = 0.60–0.69 is considered marginal and reportable with caveats; 
α below 0.60 is considered unacceptable and would indicate the subscale requires revision before 
further use. 
5.2 Reference instruments used 
• PSS-10 — Cohen, Kamarck & Mermelstein (1983). Perceived Stress Scale, 10-item version. 
Widely validated internationally and within Indian populations. 
• DASS-21 — Lovibond & Lovibond (1995). Depression Anxiety Stress Scales, stress subscale (7 
items). 
• WHO-5 — World Health Organization (1998). Five-item Well-Being Index; public domain for 
research use; validated in 200+ published studies. 
• BRS — Smith et al. (2008). Brief Resilience Scale, 6 items. 
5.3 Sample and statistical plan 
Target sample: N = 1,000 minimum. All respondents complete MSI alongside all four comparator 
instruments in a single sitting (concurrent design). Analysis: Pearson correlation coefficients (subscale-
to-comparator and total-to-PSS-10), Cronbach's alpha per subscale and total, and exploratory factor 
analysis to examine whether the 16 items empirically cluster into the four intended subscales. 
5.4 Implications of N = 1,000 
A sample of this size is well beyond the minimum needed for stable correlation and reliability 
estimates (N = 100–200 is typically sufficient for those alone) and unlocks analyses that are not reliable 
at smaller samples: 

=== PAGE 6 ===
• Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), not just exploratory (EFA) — at N ≥ 1,000, the four-
subscale structure can be formally tested for fit (e.g., via CFI, RMSEA, TLI fit indices) rather 
than only explored, which is a materially stronger form of structural validity evidence. 
• A train/test split — the sample can be randomly divided (e.g., 60/40), running EFA on one half 
and confirmatory CFA on the other, which avoids the methodological criticism of fitting and 
confirming a structure on the same data. 
• Sub-group analysis with adequate power — correlations and reliability can be checked 
separately by gender, student vs. working professional, and other demographic splits without 
each subgroup becoming underpowered. 
• Tighter confidence intervals on every reported statistic — an r = 0.50 at N = 1,000 has a much 
narrower, more defensible confidence interval than the same r at N = 200. 
One caution worth flagging directly: at N = 1,000, statistical significance (p < .05) becomes very easy 
to achieve even for correlations that are practically trivial — a correlation as weak as r ≈ 0.06 can be 
"statistically significant" at this sample size despite explaining under 1% of shared variance. The 
Cohen's-d style benchmarks in Section 5 (weak/moderate/strong) — not the p-value alone — should 
be the basis for any claim of validity. A subscale correlating with its comparator at r = 0.15 and p < 
.001 should still be reported and treated as a weak, not a meaningful, relationship. 
A second point worth deciding deliberately: reaching N = 1,000 will very likely require recruitment 
beyond a single university's WhatsApp/campus channels alone. If the final sample remains 
substantially concentrated within Parul University, the "single convenience sample" limitation noted 
in Section 8 still applies regardless of the raw N — a large but narrow sample does not, by itself, 
generalise more broadly than a small one. If broader recruitment (multiple institutions, working 
professionals outside the university, etc.) is part of the plan to reach 1,000, that should be 
documented as part of the sampling strategy, since it materially strengthens the generalisability claim 
you can make. 
6. Final Scoring Matrix 
Band Composite Score Range Status 
Healthy 0–20 Provisional* 
Mild 21–40 Provisional* 
Moderate 41–60 Provisional*

Burnout 81–100 Provisional* 
  
*These five bands are currently evenly-spaced placeholders and have not yet been empirically 
derived from validation data. The scientifically defensible approach — to be completed once 
validation data is collected — is to set band boundaries using either (a) percentile-based cutoffs 
from the actual response distribution, or (b) anchoring against PSS-10's own published severity 
cutoffs via the regression relationship established during validation. Presenting these bands as final 
or clinically meaningful prior to this step would not be methodologically defensible and should be 
avoided in any external-facing material.

8. Known Limitations (to be addressed post-validation) 
• Band cutoffs (Section 6) are provisional and not yet empirically derived — this must be 
corrected before any external stress-level claims are made. 
• Recovery & Support, even at 4 items, may show a weaker correlation with its comparator 
(BRS) than other subscales, given resilience/social-support constructs are less directly 
captured by the existing gold standards used elsewhere in this study. 
• Validation sample is a convenience sample from a single university; findings should be 
described as preliminary and population-specific until replicated in a broader or more 
representative sample. 
• MSI measures self-reported, perceived stress only. It does not measure or infer any 
biological/physiological stress marker (e.g., cortisol), and should never be described or 
marketed as a biomarker-based or clinical diagnostic instrument.
